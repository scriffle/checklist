TITLE: I can explain the difference between an electric monopole and an electric dipole
CODE: 1.5

WHAT (concept explanation):
An electric monopole is a single isolated charge — either positive (field lines pointing outward) or negative (field lines pointing inward). An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges separated by a short distance, with field lines running from the positive charge to the negative charge.

WHY (importance):
The monopole/dipole distinction is part of the study design glossary and connects to comparisons with gravitational fields (mass is always a gravitational monopole) and magnetic fields (magnetic dipoles have no monopole equivalent). It also has real-world relevance in molecular chemistry.

EXAMPLE (concrete illustration):
A single electron is an electric monopole — its field lines all point inward. A water molecule (H₂O) behaves as an electric dipole because oxygen pulls shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, creating a partial negative charge on the oxygen end and partial positive charges on the hydrogen ends.

SUBTOPIC: Electric Field Concepts
SUBTOPIC CONTEXT: This subtopic covers the definition of an electric field as a vector field, the rules for drawing electric field line diagrams around point charges and between parallel plates, and the distinction between electric monopoles and dipoles. It also covers how electric fields compare to gravitational fields.
SUBTOPIC RELEVANCE: The field model is the conceptual foundation for all electric force and energy calculations. Understanding how to draw and interpret field line diagrams is a required practical and exam skill, and distinguishing monopoles from dipoles connects electric fields to both magnetic fields and real-world molecular structures.

TITLE: I can explain the difference between an electric monopole and an electric dipole
CODE: 1.5

WHAT (concept explanation):
An electric monopole is a single isolated charge — either positive (field lines pointing outward) or negative (field lines pointing inward). An electric dipole consists of two equal and opposite charges separated by a short distance, with field lines running from the positive charge to the negative charge.

WHY (importance):
The monopole/dipole distinction is part of the study design glossary and connects to comparisons with gravitational fields (mass is always a gravitational monopole) and magnetic fields (magnetic dipoles have no monopole equivalent). It also has real-world relevance in molecular chemistry.

EXAMPLE (concrete illustration):
A single electron is an electric monopole — its field lines all point inward. A water molecule (H₂O) behaves as an electric dipole because oxygen pulls shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does, creating a partial negative charge on the oxygen end and partial positive charges on the hydrogen ends.

SUBTOPIC: Electric Field Concepts
SUBTOPIC CONTEXT: This subtopic covers the definition of an electric field as a vector field, the rules for drawing electric field line diagrams around point charges and between parallel plates, and the distinction between electric monopoles and dipoles. It also covers how electric fields compare to gravitational fields.
SUBTOPIC RELEVANCE: The field model is the conceptual foundation for all electric force and energy calculations. Understanding how to draw and interpret field line diagrams is a required practical and exam skill, and distinguishing monopoles from dipoles connects electric fields to both magnetic fields and real-world molecular structures.
